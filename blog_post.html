Why I Decided to Build an LLM from Scratch

Let me be honest with you: when I first heard people say "I trained my own language model," it sounded like witchcraft. ChatGPT, GPT-4, LLaMA — these things have billions of parameters and cost millions to train. How could anyone build one at home?

But here's the thing that changed my perspective: the core ideas behind these models are surprisingly elegant. The math isn't alien. The architecture fits in a few hundred lines of Python. And you absolutely can build a tiny version on your laptop — one that actually learns to produce (vaguely) coherent text — in under two minutes.

So I did exactly that. I built a 1.3-million-parameter GPT-style model, trained it on children's stories, and watched it go from producing random gibberish to stringing together real-ish words. It's not going to write your essays, but the journey of building it taught me more about how LLMs work than any tutorial or YouTube video ever did.

Let me walk you through everything — no PhD required, I promise.

What Is a Transformer, Really?

Forget the jargon for a second. Imagine you're reading a sentence: "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired."

When you get to the word "it," your brain instantly knows "it" refers to "the cat," not "the mat." You don't think about this consciously — your brain just pays attention to the right words based on context.

That's basically what a Transformer does. It's a machine that reads a sequence of words and, for every word, figures out which other words are most important for understanding it. The whole trick is in that word: attention.

Before Transformers came along in 2017 (in the famous paper "Attention Is All You Need"), language models had to read words one-by-one, left to right, like a person reading under a spotlight that can only see one word at a time. Transformers changed the game by letting the model see the entire sentence at once and decide what to focus on.

🍕 Pizza analogy: Think of older models (RNNs) as eating a pizza one bite at a time in order — you can't taste slice 5 until you've finished slices 1-4. A Transformer is like looking at the entire pizza, deciding which slices look best, and eating them in any order. Way more efficient.

The Attention Mechanism: The Secret Sauce

Okay, so here's the cool part — the attention mechanism is basically like a search engine inside the model.

Every word in a sentence asks three questions about itself:

  • Query (Q): "What kind of information am I looking for?" — like typing a search query into Google.
  • Key (K): "What kind of information do I have?" — like the title/tags on a web page.
  • Value (V): "Here's my actual content." — like the actual content on that web page.

When the model processes the word "it" in our cat sentence, the Query for "it" is something like "I need to know what noun I'm referring to." The Key for "cat" is basically advertising "I'm an animate noun, a subject." The dot product between these two vectors is high — it's a match! — so "it" pays a lot of attention to "cat."

A Tiny Concrete Example

Let me make this super concrete. Imagine each word has a tiny 3-number vector (in reality it's 32 or 128 numbers, but bear with me):

# Imagine these are learned vectors (3 dimensions for simplicity)
Token "cat" → Query = [0.5, 0.1, 0.8]   # "I'm looking for subjects"
Token "sat" → Key   = [0.1, 0.9, 0.2]   # "I'm a verb"
Token "it"  → Key   = [0.6, 0.2, 0.7]   # "I'm a pronoun/subject"

# Attention score = dot product of Query and Key
score(cat → sat) = 0.5×0.1 + 0.1×0.9 + 0.8×0.2 = 0.30  # Low match
score(cat → it)  = 0.5×0.6 + 0.1×0.2 + 0.8×0.7 = 0.88  # High match!

See that? "Cat" naturally pays way more attention to "it" (score 0.88) than to "sat" (score 0.30). The model learns these vectors during training so the right words match up.

After computing these scores, the model runs them through softmax (a function that squashes numbers into percentages that add up to 100%), and uses those percentages to blend the Value vectors together. The result? Each word gets a new representation that's a weighted mix of the words it paid attention to.

🤔 Why divide by √d? There's one more detail: the scores are divided by the square root of the vector dimension before softmax. Without this, the dot products can get really large, and softmax would essentially say "pay 100% attention to one word, 0% to everything else." The scaling keeps things smooth so the model can attend to multiple words at once.

Multi-Head Attention: Looking at Things from Different Angles

Here's where it gets even more clever. Instead of having one big attention mechanism, my model splits it into 4 separate "heads" — four independent attention computations running in parallel.

Why? Because language has many layers of meaning. One head might learn to track what the subject of the sentence is. Another might learn which adjective modifies which noun. A third might focus on proximity — words that are close together. Having multiple heads is like having four different people reading the same sentence, each looking for different things, and then combining their notes.

Here's the actual code from my model that does this:

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_heads = cfg.n_heads           # 4 attention heads
        self.d_head = cfg.d_model // cfg.n_heads  # 128/4 = 32 dims per head

        # One matrix that produces Q, K, V all at once (efficient!)
        self.qkv_proj = nn.Linear(cfg.d_model, 3 * cfg.d_model, bias=False)
        self.out_proj = nn.Linear(cfg.d_model, cfg.d_model, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x):
        B, T, C = x.shape
        # Compute Q, K, V in a single matrix multiply
        qkv = self.qkv_proj(x)              # (B, T, 3*C)
        q, k, v = qkv.chunk(3, dim=-1)      # Split into Q, K, V

        # Reshape: split each into 4 heads of 32 dims
        q = q.view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)
        k = k.view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)
        v = v.view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)

        # The magic formula: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T / √d) × V
        scale = math.sqrt(self.d_head)
        attn = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) / scale  # Score matrix
        attn = attn.masked_fill(self.causal_mask[:T, :T], float("-inf"))
        attn = F.softmax(attn, dim=-1)       # Normalize to probabilities
        out = attn @ v                        # Weighted sum of values
        ...

I love how the entire attention mechanism — this revolutionary idea that powers ChatGPT — boils down to a dot product, a mask, a softmax, and a matrix multiply. That's it. That's the magic.

The Full Architecture: How It All Fits Together

Now let me zoom out and show you the big picture. My model is a decoder-only transformer — the same family as GPT-2, GPT-3, and LLaMA. "Decoder-only" just means it's built to predict the next word, reading left to right.

Here's the full data flow, from input to output:

Input: "Once upon a time" → Token IDs [42, 100, 7, 303] │ ▼ ┌──────────────────────┐ │ Token Embedding │ Look up a 128-dim vector for each word │ + Position Embed │ Add "where am I in the sentence?" info └──────────┬───────────┘ │ │ × 4 Transformer Blocks ┌──────────▼───────────┐ │ LayerNorm │ │ Multi-Head Attn │ Each word gathers context from past words │ + Skip Connection │ ├───────────────────────┤ │ LayerNorm │ │ Feed-Forward (MLP) │ Each word "thinks" about its context │ + Skip Connection │ └──────────┬───────────┘ │ ┌──────────▼───────────┐ │ Final LayerNorm │ │ Linear → 4000 logits│ Score for every word in vocabulary └──────────────────────┘ │ ▼ Output: probability distribution over next word

A few things worth highlighting:

  • Token Embeddings are a lookup table: each of the 4,000 words in our vocabulary gets its own 128-number vector. The model learns these during training.
  • Positional Embeddings are essential because, unlike reading word-by-word, the Transformer sees all words at once. Without position info, "cat sat mat" and "mat sat cat" would look identical.
  • Skip Connections (the "+" parts) are genius — they let information flow straight through, so each block only needs to learn a small "correction" instead of the whole representation.
  • The Feed-Forward Network expands each word's 128-dim vector to 512 dimensions, applies a nonlinear function (GELU), then compresses back to 128. This is where a lot of the model's "knowledge" gets stored.

Training: Watching a Model Learn

Training a language model is conceptually simple: show it tons of text, ask it to predict the next word, tell it how wrong it was, and nudge the parameters to be less wrong next time. Repeat a few hundred thousand times.

Here's the core training loop, stripped to its essence:

for step in range(max_steps):
    input_ids, target_ids = get_batch()      # Grab a chunk of text

    _, loss = model(input_ids, targets=target_ids)  # Forward pass
    # loss = how surprised the model is by the actual next word

    optimizer.zero_grad()   # Clear old gradients
    loss.backward()         # Backprop: compute gradients
    optimizer.step()        # Update all 1.3M parameters

Every single step, the model sees 16 sequences of 128 tokens each (that's 2,048 tokens per step). Over 500 steps, it processes about 1 million tokens total.

Under the Hood: Key Code Explained

Let me walk you through a few pieces of the code that I found most illuminating.

The Heart of the Model

The SmallGPT class ties everything together. What surprised me is how clean it is:

class SmallGPT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        # Step 1: Convert token IDs → vectors
        self.token_emb = nn.Embedding(cfg.vocab_size, cfg.d_model)
        # Step 2: Add position information
        self.pos_emb = LearnedPositionalEmbedding(cfg.max_seq_len, cfg.d_model)
        # Step 3: Stack transformer blocks
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([
            TransformerBlock(cfg) for _ in range(cfg.n_layers)  # 4 blocks
        ])
        # Step 4: Final projection to vocabulary
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(cfg.d_model)
        self.lm_head = nn.Linear(cfg.d_model, cfg.vocab_size, bias=False)
        # Neat trick: share weights between embedding and output
        self.lm_head.weight = self.token_emb.weight

That last line — weight tying — is a clever trick used by GPT-2 and many modern models. The token embedding converts words→vectors, and the output layer converts vectors→word-scores. They're doing conceptually opposite things, so sharing the weights saves ~500K parameters and actually makes the model work better.

The Causal Mask: No Cheating Allowed

One detail that blew my mind is the causal mask. During training, the model sees the full sentence at once, but we need to prevent it from "cheating" — looking ahead at words it hasn't generated yet. The solution is a triangular mask:

# Create a mask where True = "block this position"
mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(seq_len, seq_len), diagonal=1).bool()

# Visualized for seq_len=4:
#   Position:     0      1      2      3
# Word 0:   [  OK    BLOCK  BLOCK  BLOCK ]  ← can only see itself
# Word 1:   [  OK     OK    BLOCK  BLOCK ]  ← sees words 0,1
# Word 2:   [  OK     OK     OK    BLOCK ]  ← sees words 0,1,2
# Word 3:   [  OK     OK     OK     OK   ]  ← sees everything

The masked positions get set to negative infinity before softmax, which means they become exactly 0% attention. Information only flows from past to present — never the other direction.

Learning Rate Warmup + Cosine Decay

One thing I didn't appreciate before building this: the learning rate schedule matters a lot. You can't just pick one number and go. Here's the schedule I used:

def get_lr(step, cfg):
    # Phase 1: Warmup (steps 0-50) — start gentle
    if step < cfg.warmup_steps:
        return cfg.learning_rate * (step / cfg.warmup_steps)

    # Phase 2: Cosine decay (steps 50-500) — gradually slow down
    progress = (step - cfg.warmup_steps) / (cfg.max_steps - cfg.warmup_steps)
    min_lr = cfg.learning_rate * 0.1  # Never go below 10% of peak
    return min_lr + 0.5 * (cfg.learning_rate - min_lr) * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

Think of it like driving: you start slowly (warmup), hit highway speed (peak learning rate), then gradually slow down as you approach your destination (cosine decay). Starting too fast at the beginning — when the model's weights are all random — causes chaos.

Lessons I Learned Along the Way

Building this project taught me a bunch of things that I didn't expect. Here are my biggest takeaways:

1. The architecture is surprisingly simple. Before this project, I thought Transformers must be incredibly complex under the hood. They're not. The core idea — Q, K, V attention — is just dot products and softmax. The genius is in how these simple pieces compose together.

2. Data matters more than model size. My model's biggest limitation wasn't its 1.3M parameters — it was the tiny dataset of children's stories. Even a small model can produce decent text if you give it enough diverse data. The big models aren't magic; they're just very well-fed.

3. Tokenization is half the battle. Before text even reaches the model, it gets chopped into sub-word tokens by the BPE tokenizer. The quality of these tokens — how well they capture meaningful chunks of language — has a huge impact on what the model can learn.

4. Loss numbers are deeply unintuitive. Going from loss 8.0 to 4.0 sounds like "cutting the error in half," but in practice it means going from "random guessing" to "knows which 57 words are plausible here." The log-scale nature of cross-entropy loss took me a while to internalize.

5. The learning rate schedule isn't optional. I tried training without warmup once and the loss spiked all over the place. The combination of warmup + cosine decay that every modern LLM uses isn't ceremony — it's genuinely important for stable training.

6. Weight tying is elegant. Sharing the embedding and output weights saves memory and actually improves performance. The intuition — that similar words should have similar embeddings AND similar output probabilities — is really satisfying once it clicks.

💡 My #1 tip for anyone starting out: Don't try to understand everything at once. Build it, run it, and watch the loss go down. Then go back and read the code more carefully. Understanding comes from doing, not just reading.
